# Annex II — Why the Minimal Case Is Extensible

- **Version:** 1.2
- **Parent case:** [Annex I — Minimal Operational Case](02_ANNEX_I_Minimal_Operational_Case.md)
- **Status:** Public pre-freeze working material; not an adopted FG-TIDA deliverable.

The case is deliberately small, but it is not deliberately rigid. Annex I defines the deliberately restricted mobility instantiation. This Annex II tests whether its core concepts survive controlled changes in scale and service domain; it does not add those extensions to the initial case commitment. Upward extension adds actors, roles and objective layers; downward extension reduces the setting to a private factory or production cell; horizontal extension changes the service domain. An extension passes only if it can be represented without redefining the core meanings of principal, role, agent, authority, preference, policy, grant, commitment and decision. This is a design test, not a claim of universality.

## 1. Upward extensibility — from the minimal case to a larger ecosystem.

The current mobility example can grow by adding principals, agents, services, role chains and jurisdictions. A citizen and municipality can become a multi-actor environment containing public transport, taxis/VTC, robotaxis or other autonomous services, private vehicles, shared fleets, parking and access providers, employers, service providers, infrastructure operators, logistics actors and several public authorities. Public authority can itself contain policy-setting roles, operational roles, supervisors and bounded exceptional authorities; private actors can likewise contain employers, employees, households, operators and several personal or organizational agents. The case remains the same if these additions are represented as additional roles, grants, constraints and commitments rather than by changing the meaning of authority or delegation.

The original extension examples remain valid. The same private dimensions — commitment/reliability, autonomy and economic preference — can describe a passenger accepting an assigned low-cost route, a driver deciding whether to carpool for compensation, a citizen accepting a fixed appointment slot, or a user retaining flexibility to decide at the last moment. The corresponding system-level dimensions — efficient use of scarce capacity, externalities and compliance with governing policies — can move from roads to appointment capacity, public-service queues, access windows, energy or other constrained resources. Upward extension therefore shows that the case is not limited to one vehicle, one service or one municipality.

Nested objectives, preferences and KPIs. The three dimensions currently shown for each side are the smallest readable starting set, not a fixed maximum. An upward extension may decompose them into nested objective systems: a city objective may lead to service, operator and agent objectives, while a principal preference may lead to bounded delegated sub-objectives. Each objective can identify its owner, scope, priority, hard or soft status, KPI, target or acceptable range, measurement source, time horizon, dependencies and escalation condition. For example, arrival within a time window may be combined with maximum detour, price, accessibility and emissions ranges. Derived objectives remain traceable to the relevant principal or mandate; optimizing a local KPI does not create authority or permit a lower-level objective to override a higher-level constraint.

## 2. Downward extensibility — a private factory as a bounded deployment.

The specific example used here is a privately operated factory reduced, if useful, to one production cell. A customer order or maintenance intervention must be completed within a defined window while workers and technical agents coordinate a machine, materials, energy, tools and maintenance time. Management sets production priorities; safety and quality roles impose non-substitutable constraints; maintenance controls machine release; and a local agent may choose sequence, method, tool or timing only inside its delegated envelope. This preserves the authority, delegation, scarce-capacity and runtime-change problem with fewer actors and without treating the factory as a city in miniature.

| Case role | Core dimension | Private-factory example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local actor / delegated agent | Commitment reliability | Complete an order or intervention within the required window |
| Local actor / delegated agent | Autonomy and flexibility | Choose sequence, method, tool or timing inside a bounded envelope |
| Local actor / delegated agent | Economic or resource preference | Avoid overtime or scarce resources; accept bounded trade-offs or incentives |
| Governing or constraint-setting role | Efficient use of shared capacity | Coordinate machines, people, materials, energy and production slots |
| Governing or constraint-setting role | Externalities | Control energy, scrap, wear, noise, safety exposure and maintenance burden |
| Governing or constraint-setting role | Policy and constraint compliance | Apply quality, safety, maintenance, production-priority and operating rules |

Concrete runtime change in the private factory. At T0, the production agent is authorized to schedule the order within the normal operating envelope. At T1, a machine fault, missing material or safety-threshold breach invalidates part of the plan. At T2, the system must determine whether the original authority and commitments still apply, which role may revise them, and whether the permitted response is to continue, reroute, hold or escalate. The supervisor, maintenance function and safety or quality role govern different action domains; none is a universal higher authority. The test is whether their bounded authority can be composed into one current determination for the specific action.

## 3. Horizontal extensibility — change the domain, keep the structural problem.

The model should also move laterally across domains without necessarily increasing or reducing scale. Mobility can be replaced by appointment scheduling, a school allocating rooms and specialist time, a hospital department coordinating beds and staff, an enterprise allocating compute or specialist capacity, or a logistics operation coordinating loads and delivery windows. The concrete resources and policies change, but the recurring structure remains: actors have objectives; resources or service capacity are finite; some decisions are delegated to agents; commitments are created; authority is bounded and may overlap; and runtime events can invalidate an earlier plan or require a different authority path.

What must remain invariant across extension. Changing scale or domain is allowed; silently changing the semantics is not. A principal remains the source of an interest or mandate; a role remains a bounded function; an agent acts under delegated authority rather than acquiring authority from convenience; a preference remains distinguishable from a permission; a policy or organizational rule remains distinguishable from the grant that makes it operative for a given action; and a commitment remains something whose standing may need to be re-evaluated when context changes. These invariants are what make the same case testable at city, factory-cell and service-system scale.

A useful boundary signal. Extension has failed when a new situation cannot be represented by adding actors, roles, grants, constraints, resources or commitments without redefining those concepts. For example, if a class of agentic interaction has no meaningful principal, no bounded authority relation or no identifiable decision/commitment point, it may require a different frozen case. Recording that boundary is part of the extensibility result; it is preferable to forcing the present model to appear universal.

The extensibility test is therefore three-sided. Can the case grow without changing its core semantics? Can it shrink without losing the authority, delegation and coordination problem it is meant to expose? Can it move into a different domain while preserving the same distinctions between principals, roles, agents, preferences, policies, grants, commitments and decisions? If the answer becomes no for an important class of agentic situations, that limit should be documented and a different frozen case should be created rather than forcing this one to become universal. The objective is the smallest case that remains useful across a meaningful range of agentic systems — not one case that pretends to represent all of them.
